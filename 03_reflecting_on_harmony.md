# Reflecting on harmony

Here are just a few notes and questions from my guitar lesson and practice that I've been reflecting on lately. I want to understand everything he said to the bones.


## How can a string vibrate at multiple frequencies at the same time?

When you pluck a string, multiple frequencies ring out at the same time, the fundamental and its partials. But if the string is busy moving through space, how can it produce multiple frequencies? The answer is that the the string isn't a rigid object, it is deforming and changing shape as it moves creating an arc with sub-arcs. The motion of the string creates a complex air pressure pattern and that pattern contains multiple frequencies.


## What happens when you play a harmonic?

When you play a harmonic, you briefly touch a specific point on the string (like the 12th fret) while plucking, then immediately lift off. Here's what's happening physically: while your finger touches the 12th fret, it physically holds that point in place—it can't move. When you pluck, you pull the string to give it energy, but the 12th fret stays put because your finger is there. The string bends into a shape where the section between the nut and your finger moves, and the section between your finger and the bridge moves, but your finger's location remains still. When you lift your finger, the string continues vibrating with those two sections moving back and forth—often in opposite directions (left section swings right while right section swings left). Crucially, the 12th fret location doesn't need to move for this vibration to continue; it naturally stays still as part of this vibration pattern.

Your brief touch doesn't create anything permanent—it sets up the initial conditions by forcing the string to start vibrating in a pattern where that point is still. This blocks vibration patterns that would need that point to move (like the fundamental, which requires the entire string to swing as one). By blocking these patterns, all the plucking energy flows into vibration patterns that can have a still point at your touch location. For the 12th fret, that's the 2nd harmonic (where the string vibrates in two sections at 2× frequency), along with other even harmonics. Once that pattern starts, it continues on its own because it's a stable way for the string to vibrate—the node (still point) at the 12th fret is a natural feature of this vibration pattern, not something your finger was maintaining. The string isn't "shortened," but the pitch changes because you're hearing a higher overtone that was always present in the string's complex vibration, now isolated and amplified.


## What makes a harmony sound consonant or dissonant?

When notes with simple frequency ratios, like 5:4, their overtones align well and they sound blended. This is consonance. When notes have complex frequency ratios, like 45:32, their partials clash more, creating dissonance: tension, roughness or instability.

# What do they call that feeling of "back-and-forth" between two notes in a harmony? Like a minor has a faster back-and-forth than a major to me. Do you know what I mean?

Two waves with slightly different frequencies continuously drift through different phase relationships. When they're in phase (peaks aligned), you hear louder sound. When they're out of phase (peaks opposing), you hear quieter sound. This cycle repeats, creating the pulsing "beating" effect.

## How do you count intervals?

An octave is 8 notes (C to C). A 9th is one note beyond the octave. A 13 is five notes beyone the octave (C to A but an octave highter).


## What is timbre (pronounced TAM-ber)?

Timbre (pronounced TAM-ber) is the quality that makes instruments sound different even when they're playing the same note at the same volume. Basically, timbre is the overtone fingerprint. First we have the spectrum, which is what frequenes are present and how loud. Then the specific pattern of which overtones are present and their relative amplitudes (how much the wave displaces) is the spectral signature of that sound. Another important part of timbre is how the overtones change over time -- which ones decay quickly, which ones sustain, and how the overall sound evolves from attack to release. Initial attack has complexity and noise, as you sustain, things stabalize. That's why when I sing a note and hold it for a long time, it sounds more "pure" than when I started. Interestingly, my favorite rock singer, Jeff Buckley, uses the characteristics of his attack as an artistic device.

## What shoudl I focus on in my personal practice time?

Recently I've been trying to cook up the perfect blend of exercises for my practice routine, but in the end I decided that I don't need to focus on technique, I need to focus on expression. And, in the process of expression, I will naturally gain technique.

1. Try to tell a story. Pick a story and use what you know to communicate it as best you can.

2. Practice dynamics: time, volume, pitch, spread vs condense.

3. Learn songs (or other existing music).


## What are some practice exercises that I should take away from my lesson with Maurice?

1. Harmonzing scales with triads. (Use the diatonic arps feature of fretboard composer pro for this, but also check out Jens Larsen.)

2. Identify the intervals in my chord progression (E5 -> D6/9 -> Cmaj7 -> B5add11) and identify what interval the open b and e strings create over each bass note. Write it down. Then learn to play that interval in different positions on the neck, and learn what it sounds like in other contexts. This is a way to learn "flavors".

3. Experiment with how many different vocal lines I can come up with over a pedal tone. Explore how different intervals creat different emotional colors. Whare are some colorful departures from just harmonizing using the root note at different octaves?

4. Experiment with learning the tablature that Maurice gave me.

5. Analyze the harmony of my church songs.
